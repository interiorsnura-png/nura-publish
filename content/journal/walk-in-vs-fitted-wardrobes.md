# A Walk-In Wardrobe or a Wall of Fitted Wardrobes: Which Stores More?

A walk-in wardrobe can store more when its layout creates additional usable cabinetry. Whether that is the better use of a bedroom depends on the clothes you own and the space you give up to reach them.

In the worked example below, the walk-in arrangement has twice the usable hanging length and twice the drawers of the wardrobe wall. It also separates a substantial part of the room from the sleeping area. The same clothing collection fits in the simpler wardrobe wall already.

For a London bedroom project, that is the useful comparison: how much accessible storage each plan provides, what you actually need, and how the rest of the room works afterwards.

## First, compare the layouts

“Walk-in” describes a space you enter; “fitted” describes furniture installed to suit its position. A walk-in wardrobe can contain fitted wardrobes. Here, both options use fitted cabinetry:

- **Plan A:** one continuous wardrobe bank against the bedroom wall.
- **Plan B:** the same bank, a second bank facing it, and a partition creating a walk-in zone.

This is a comparison of those two arrangements. It does not establish that every walk-in stores more than every wardrobe wall.

## One room and one unchanged brief

**This is an educational scenario, not a completed NURA project or a surveyed property.** Every dimension, garment allowance and access assumption below is chosen for the example. The 27 m² room is deliberately generous; it is not presented as a typical London bedroom.

| Fixed element | Identical in both plans |
| --- | --- |
| Internal room | 6,000 × 4,500 mm; sufficient ceiling height for 2,400 mm cabinetry |
| Room door | 900 mm opening in the south wall, with the same inward swing |
| Window | 1,200 mm opening in the east wall; assumed sliding, without an inward swing |
| Bed | 1,800 × 2,100 mm overall footprint, in the same position |
| Bedside tables | Two 500 × 500 mm tables, in the same positions |
| Cabinet module | 800 mm wide × 650 mm deep × 2,400 mm high |
| Front arrangement | Open wardrobe bays and exposed, flush drawer fronts in both layouts |

The bed dimensions include the assumed frame footprint. No extra room, smaller bed or removed bedside table helps either option win. The partition in Plan B is the proposed change.

Both layouts deliberately use open fronts so that the front style stays constant. Hinged doors, sliding tracks, projecting handles or a door to the walk-in would require another access check.

## The two plans at the same scale

![Two equal-scale plans of the same 6,000 by 4,500 mm bedroom: one wardrobe wall and a two-bank walk-in zone, retaining the bed, bedside tables, entrance and window.](/assets/selected/nura_wardrobe_plans_wide.png)

*Educational plans. Dimensions are in millimetres. Both drawings use the same scale. Open drawer and standing areas show one operating condition, not a permanently occupied footprint.*

### Plan A — a wall of fitted wardrobes

The west wall accommodates five 800 mm modules and a 50 mm filler at each end: **4,100 mm in total**. The bank is 650 mm deep.

From south to north, the modules contain long hanging, double short hanging, shelving, shelving, and drawers with shelves above. The space in front remains part of the bedroom and also serves as the wardrobe access area. It must stay clear when the storage is used.

### Plan B — a walk-in containing fitted wardrobes

The west bank stays exactly as it is. Four more modules face it, with the same end fillers, creating a second bank **3,300 mm long**. These contain long hanging, double short hanging, shelving, and drawers with shelves above.

The section across the walk-in is:

**650 mm cabinetry + 1,150 mm aisle + 650 mm cabinetry + 100 mm partition = 2,550 mm.**

The partition leaves a 900 mm entrance at its south end. The second bank begins farther north to allow entry. There is no island and no corner cupboard counted twice.

## The clothing collection stays the same

The hypothetical household has 12 long garments, 30 short garments, 12 folded stacks, six shoe boxes and eight drawer organisers containing smaller clothing items.

For calculation only, each long garment receives 50 mm of rail and each short garment 40 mm. The required rail lengths are therefore **600 mm for long hanging and 1,200 mm for short hanging**. The assumed drops below the rail are 1,300 mm and 750 mm respectively. These allowances are not recommendations for every coat, shirt or dress: measure your own clothes on their usual hangers.

Each folded stack measures 300 × 350 × 200 mm; each shoe box is 300 × 350 × 180 mm. Two sit side by side on a shelf, one row deep. Eighteen groups need nine shelves. The eight organisers measure 300 × 400 × 120 mm each; two fit in each of four drawers.

The broader [NURA joinery planning guide](https://www.nura-interiors.com/guides/bespoke-joinery-london) explains how to turn the items you own and the way you use them into a furniture brief.

## Count usable storage inside the cabinets

An 800 mm module does not provide an 800 mm rail. In this example, each independently boxed module has two 18 mm sides:

**800 − 18 − 18 = 764 mm internal width.**

Allowing a further 12 mm at each end for the rail fittings leaves **740 mm of usable hanging length**. Shelf surfaces are 760 × 500 mm after the chosen fitting gaps. Each drawer has a clear interior of 690 × 450 × 160 mm; runners and drawer-box sides are excluded from that measurement.

The smallest clear height above any counted everyday shelf is **232 mm**: the underside of the 18 mm high shelf at 2,050 mm sits above the long-hanging module's shelf at 1,800 mm. This accommodates the stated 200 mm stacks and 180 mm boxes within the model. The 1,700 mm long rail also allows the assumed hanger hook to rise 50 mm without meeting the shelf above it. Actual retrieval space and reach still need checking with the users.

The module schedule makes the comparison reproducible:

| Module | Daily-use storage | Plan A | Plan B |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| L — long hanging | One 740 mm rail and one shelf | 1 | 2 |
| D — double short hanging | Two 740 mm rails | 1 | 2 |
| S — shelving | Four shelves | 2 | 3 |
| R — drawers and shelves | Four drawers and two shelves | 1 | 2 |

Every module also has a shelf at 2,050 mm. These high shelves are reported separately and excluded from daily-use capacity. The everyday shelves have surfaces between 400 and 1,800 mm; the example assumes users who can reach the specified contents. A lower reach requirement would change the usable count.

| Measure | Plan A — wardrobe wall | Plan B — walk-in |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Usable long-hanging rail | 0.74 m | 1.48 m |
| Usable short-hanging rail | 1.48 m across two rails | 2.96 m across four rails |
| Daily-use shelves | 11 | 18 |
| Daily-use shelf surface | 4.18 m² | 6.84 m² |
| Drawers that can open fully individually | 4 | 8 |
| High shelves excluded from daily total | 5 | 9 |
| Does the unchanged collection fit? | Yes | Yes |

The shelf areas are **11 × 0.760 × 0.500** and **18 × 0.760 × 0.500**. They describe horizontal shelf surfaces, not room floor area or a guaranteed quantity of clothing.

Long and short hanging stay separate because two short rails cannot replace a full-length space for a long dress. Here, the short rails sit at 900 and 1,800 mm; their 750 mm garment drops fit the chosen arrangement. The long rail sits at 1,700 mm. These heights should change if the garments or users require it.

Plan A already accommodates the inventory on nine of its eleven everyday shelves, all four drawers and part of its available rail length. Plan B adds capacity, but the brief does not currently need the extra bank. That distinction matters more than the total number of cupboards.

## Can the drawers open while someone uses them?

An empty aisle measurement answers only part of the question. The drawer needs to travel out, and the person using it needs somewhere to stand.

For this model, a drawer projects **500 mm beyond its closed face**. We reserve a **600 mm standing depth**, giving an operating requirement of **1,100 mm**. These are selected assumptions, not universal comfort or accessibility standards.

In Plan A, that operating strip fits in front of the bank without meeting the bed or bedside furniture. Across from the drawer module, the closest bedside table starts 2,100 mm beyond the cabinet face. Opening a 500 mm drawer and reserving 600 mm for its user leaves 1,000 mm beyond that operating space at this location.

In Plan B, the 1,150 mm aisle accommodates the same drawer and user with only **50 mm left in the model**. That is a tight result which needs checking with the actual person and fittings. With opposite drawers extended, only **150 mm** remains between their fronts: 1,150 − 500 − 500. The arrangement cannot support the same standing allowance then.

The access count therefore means **eight drawers usable one at a time**, not eight drawers usable simultaneously. Two assumed 600 mm-wide users also cannot pass side by side in the 1,150 mm aisle.

At specification stage, check the actual hinges, runners and front positions. [Blum’s Cabinet Configurator](https://www.blum.com/gb/en/services/planning-construction-product-selection/cabinet-configurator/) includes collision checking for cabinet and fitting arrangements. Our educational room model is not a manufacturer-approved cabinet design.

## What remains of the bedroom?

The extra storage is created by adding furniture and reserving a separate area for dressing. Floor area needs two descriptions: what solid objects occupy, and where the remaining clear floor belongs. These area calculations take all drawers as closed; an open drawer temporarily occupies part of the clear floor.

| Floor measure, rounded | Plan A | Plan B |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cabinetry and partition footprint | 2.67 m² | 5.17 m² |
| Unoccupied floor across the whole room | 20.06 m² | 17.55 m² |
| Of that, inside the dedicated walk-in zone | None | 6.31 m² |
| Of that, outside the walk-in zone | 20.06 m² | 11.25 m² |

*Areas are rounded independently, so displayed components may differ from the total by 0.01 m².*

Both calculations deduct the same **4.28 m²** for the bed and bedside tables. In Plan B, the entire allocated walk-in zone is **2.55 × 4.50 = 11.475 m²**, including its cabinetry, partition and clear floor. Those components are not additional areas to add on top.

“Unoccupied” does not mean available for another piece of furniture. It includes circulation, the room-door swing and wardrobe operating space. In Plan A, a continuous 1,100 mm-deep operating strip along the 4,100 mm bank alone accounts for 4.51 m² of that shared floor.

The unchanged bed has a 750 mm gap to the new partition along its west side. This narrows to 200 mm beside the bedside table at the head of the bed; that small gap is **not a passage**. The table is approached from its front. The room entrance and the route to the window remain clear in the model.

For someone who values a separate dressing area, Plan B makes that separation tangible. Someone who wants a more open bedroom may prefer Plan A, especially when the same clothes already fit.

## Which arrangement should you choose for your room?

In these two plans, **the walk-in stores more in absolute terms**. The additional bank provides another long rail, two short rails, seven everyday shelves and four drawers. The wardrobe wall meets the present storage requirement while preserving more open bedroom floor.

A different inventory could change the decision. More long coats might require a shelving module to become long hanging. A collection of deeper shoes could invalidate the shelf packing. A lower reach range could remove a shelf from the everyday count. Recalculate the schedule after each change.

The room can change the answer too. A chimney breast, an inward-opening window, a radiator or a sloping ceiling may interrupt one of the banks. If a proposed walk-in merely adds a partition around the existing bank, the partition itself adds no storage. The benefit must come from usable cabinetry, access or the way the room supports your routine.

These plans are two workable arrangements under stated assumptions, rather than a search for the maximum possible storage layout. Before choosing for your own home, ask for both alternatives on the same drawing, with the bed footprint, openings, clothes and access requirements retained.

Use the [joinery site survey guide](https://www.nura-interiors.com/post/joinery-site-survey) to distinguish measured room conditions from proposed finished dimensions. Then mark what each design holds and what must remain clear. A rendered view can help you judge the appearance; the dimensioned plan and storage schedule explain how you would use it.
